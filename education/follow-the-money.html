How we follow the money

Public education · how The Loeb Network traces money through public records.

Following someone’s money tells you a great deal. It shows what they are involved in, who they do business with, and who they may answer to. Money leaves a paper trail, and much of that trail sits in public records that anyone can look up.

Documented money trails have broken major cases. In Pennsylvania’s “Kids for Cash” scandal, payments from the developer of juvenile detention facilities to a judge were central to his conviction. In Malaysia, money traced from the state fund 1MDB and a related company into a former prime minister’s personal accounts led to his convictions.

This page explains how we do it, which free public sources we use, and where the limits are.

The method

Every inquiry follows the same steps. Each step builds on the one before it.

  1. Identify the person or entity. We start with exact names: the full legal name, known aliases, business names and addresses. Getting this right first prevents mixing up two people who happen to share a name.
  2. Map companies and roles. Business filings show which companies a person formed, runs or serves as an officer or registered agent for, and how those companies connect to one another.
  3. Check property. Deeds, mortgages, liens and other recorded documents show who bought or sold what, when, for how much, and through which company or lender.
  4. Follow political money. Campaign finance filings show who gave to whom, and who received money from whom, at the federal, state and local level.
  5. Look at contracts and lobbying. Government contract and grant records, together with lobbying disclosures, show where public money went and who was paid to influence the people who spend it.
  6. Review nonprofits. A charity’s annual IRS Form 990 lists its revenue, its top salaries, its board and its related organizations.
  7. Search court records. Lawsuits, judgments, bankruptcies and criminal cases often contain sworn testimony and financial exhibits that appear nowhere else.
  8. Connect the dots. We lay the findings side by side on a timeline: a donation, then a contract; a new company, then a property purchase. A pattern is a reason to ask questions. It is not a conclusion.
  9. Document every link. Every connection we report is tied to a specific record that anyone can look up. If we can’t cite it, we don’t publish it.

Free public sources

These are the official and nonprofit databases we turn to most. All of them can be searched without a paid account, though a few charge small fees for copies of documents.

Campaign money

  • FEC campaign finance data: Federal Election Commission filings: contributions to and spending by federal candidates, parties and PACs.
  • OpenSecrets: Federal campaign and lobbying money, organized by donor, industry and politician.
  • NY State Board of Elections: Public Reporting: Campaign finance disclosures for New York State and local candidates and committees.

Companies and corporate filings

  • NYS Division of Corporations: Corporation and Business Entity Database: New York corporations, LLCs, partnerships and not-for-profit corporations, with filing history and service addresses.
  • SEC EDGAR full-text search: Filings by publicly traded companies and their insiders.
  • OpenCorporates: A searchable index of company registrations from many countries and U.S. states.

Government contracts and lobbying

  • USAspending.gov: Federal contracts, grants and loans: who received them and how much.
  • SAM.gov: Federal contractor registrations, contract opportunities and exclusion (debarment) records.
  • LDA.gov: Lobbying Disclosure Act reports: Federal lobbying registrations, quarterly activity reports and lobbyist contribution reports. Senate and House filings are now searched in this one database.

Nonprofits

  • ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer: IRS Form 990 filings for tax-exempt organizations: revenue, salaries and officers.
  • IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search: The IRS’s own tool for confirming an organization’s tax-exempt status and viewing its filed returns.

Courts

  • PACER: Federal court dockets and filings. Searching is open to anyone; documents carry a small per-page fee, waived for light users.
  • CourtListener: A free nonprofit search of federal and state court opinions, plus many federal dockets mirrored from PACER.
  • NYSCEF (NY State Courts Electronic Filing): Filed documents in many New York State court cases.
  • WebCivil Supreme: Case information and appearances for civil cases in New York State Supreme Court.

Property

  • ACRIS (NYC Department of Finance): Deeds, mortgages and other recorded property documents for the five boroughs of New York City.
  • Suffolk County Clerk: Online Records: Suffolk County land records. Searching the index is free; viewing document images requires a paid subscription.
  • Nassau County Clerk: Land Records Search: The Nassau County Clerk’s official online land records search. Searching is free; document images carry a per-page fee. (Clerk’s office: nassaucountyny.gov)

Public payroll and spending

  • Open Book New York (NYS Comptroller): State and local government spending, state contracts and payments from the New York State Comptroller.
  • SeeThroughNY: Payroll, pension and spending data for New York public employers.

Offshore

  • ICIJ Offshore Leaks Database: Offshore companies and trusts named in the Panama Papers, Paradise Papers, Pandora Papers and related investigations. Appearing in it is not, by itself, evidence of wrongdoing.

The limits

Public records only go so far. Bank records, brokerage statements and private accounts are not public. Getting them takes a subpoena or a court order, which only courts, prosecutors and parties to a lawsuit can obtain.

So we publish only what the records show. We do not speculate, and we label every allegation as an allegation. A pattern in the records is a reason to ask questions, not proof that anyone did anything wrong. Everyone named on this site is presumed innocent unless and until a court decides otherwise.

We also hold a firm line on how records are obtained. We do not hack accounts, we do not use pretexting (pretending to be someone else to get information), and we do not buy private data.
