A founder driven by accountability

Christopher M. Loeb is the founder of The Loeb Network, which he runs as Christopher M. Loeb Solutions: a records research, advocacy, and global accountability research practice built on a simple aim, to bring balance and order through documented truth. His work centers on careful record-gathering, clear presentation of evidence, and a steady insistence that power should answer to the public.

Christopher’s commitment grows from his own experience with the justice system. It left him convinced that ordinary people deserve to be heard by the institutions meant to serve them, and that the surest way to be heard is with the documents in order. Today he helps others do exactly that. He helps people request and organize their own records and the public court filings in their matter, reconstructs timelines, and presents sourced findings through the appropriate channels. Every claim carries its source, and every person named is presumed innocent unless and until adjudicated.

A family legacy of building

Christopher is the grandson of Grumman engineer Frederick Joseph Loeb. According to family account, Frederick worked at Grumman during the Lunar Module era on the development of lightweight landing craft. That spirit of precision, patience, and doing hard things carefully is one Christopher hopes to carry into every file The Loeb Network opens.

How Christopher works

Christopher approaches every matter the same way: gather the documents, build the timeline, separate what is proven from what is alleged, and present it all transparently. He believes that fairness to everyone involved — including those accused — is what gives accountability its strength.

“To bring balance and order through documented truth — protecting the hive, one sourced fact at a time.”
— The Loeb Network mission

His story

In his words

Christopher’s own account. This section is Christopher M. Loeb’s story as he tells it, and it reflects his perspective. The court record behind it is summarized in The record at a glance, with every source listed at the bottom of this page.

Christopher M. Loeb did not build The Loeb Network in a classroom. He built it in the wreckage of a night that should never have happened — and in the long years after, when the system that was supposed to protect him tried instead to erase him.

On December 14, 2012, in a Suffolk County interrogation room, the highest-ranking uniformed officer in the department walked in on a handcuffed man and made war on him. James Burke punched and kicked and kneed. He shook Loeb’s head. He threatened a “hot shot” — a lethal dose of heroin — knowing addiction was already a knife at Loeb’s throat. When Loeb, still chained to the floor, called him what the stolen bag had already made obvious, Burke went further. Detectives finally said, Boss, that’s enough. It was not enough for the cover-up that followed.

What came next was not a single crime. It was a climate. Meetings to get stories straight. Pressure. Retaliation feared like weather. A district attorney’s office that, a federal jury later found, helped keep the truth underground. For years the official story was that nothing had happened — that a chief could beat a shackled man and walk home in his badge.

Loeb refused that story. He said it in filings. He said it when almost no one believed a “junkie” over a decorated chief. He said it again in federal court on November 2, 2016, looking Burke in the eye: I thought you were untouchable. Now look at us both. He told the court that Burke had laughed when he asked for a lawyer — this isn’t Law & Order — and that, for the first time in his life, he felt the opposite might be true: that day, there was law, and there was order.

Burke pleaded guilty to violating Loeb’s civil rights and conspiring to obstruct justice. He went to federal prison. Thomas Spota and Christopher McPartland were convicted for their part in the obstruction; the Second Circuit left those convictions standing. Loeb’s own tainted plea was vacated in January 2017, and that November a judge dismissed the charges for good and barred prosecutors from ever bringing them again. He was exonerated. His civil-rights case ended in a settlement of $1.5 million — money that could not buy back the years, the funerals missed, the grandmother he did not get to sit with, or the feeling of safety in the county he once called home.

He is not a saint in this telling, and he will not pretend to be. He has said in open court that he is not perfect — that addiction and theft were real, and that he is sorry for the harm that was his. What he will not accept is the lie that petty crime licenses torture by the state, or that power gets to write the ending.

The Loeb Network is what he built from that refusal. It investigates, documents and follows the money: public records, court filings and sourced timelines for people the system would rather bury. It does not practice law, and it does not promise miracles. It promises clarity, because clarity is the first thing a corrupt system steals.

Loeb still lives with the knowledge that badges can be weapons and that paperwork can be a weapon too. He also lives with proof that a single thread, pulled hard enough, can unravel a palace. The Loeb Network is that thread, turned into a mission: so the next person chained to a floor — or simply buried in a file — is not alone in the dark.

Why The Loeb Network exists — Judge Efman, 2013

His account

In 2013, before Judge Efman in Suffolk County, Christopher felt it was unfair that, when pretrial hearings began, the court warned the plea offer would go up significantly if he did not take it. On the record he said words to this effect: “I'm just trying to exercise my rights to a pretrial and feel that being pressured into taking a plea is coercive — and I am currently a main witness in the federal investigation into Burke.” By his account, the judge kept the offer where it stood, at three and a half years. That experience is one of the main reasons he built The Loeb Network: so people facing charges can understand their pretrial rights, discovery deadlines and plea pressure without freezing.

This is Christopher’s account and has not been independently verified. The November 2017 court decision confirms that the Hon. Martin I. Efman conducted the 2013 suppression hearing in his case [S-26].
